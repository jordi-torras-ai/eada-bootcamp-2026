Quick read

In one sentence: Newer and lower-mileage cars tend to cost more, but brand/model differences are essential; the average alone does not describe this dataset well.
  • The median is about $10,063, well below the $15,781 mean, because a smaller number of expensive cars pull the average upward.
  • Prices range from $1,249 to $131,850; the upper 1% starts around $85,854.
  • Age and mileage each have a moderate negative linear association with price (about −0.60). Horsepower has a moderate positive association (+0.52).
  • On the 1,000-row holdout, model RMSE is $1,974 (MSE $3,896,417) and MAE is $1,060; interpretation and caveats are below.

Data quality and interpretation

  • There are 50,000 records and 25 columns. Price, car age, make/model, mileage, condition, fuel and transmission have no blank values.
  • PricePerKm is missing for 2,459 records (4.9%). It is also calculated using price and mileage, so including it as an input could leak the answer into the model. The estimator correctly does not use it.
  • Year and CarAge are redundant: their correlation is −1. Keep one, not both, in a linear model to avoid duplicated information.
  • City groups are close to evenly represented, but location-specific currency/market context is absent. The dataset mixes cities from several countries while the price field carries no explicit currency metadata.
  • Condition in the CSV is categorical (New / Used / Damaged), not an observed 1–5 rating. The UI’s 1–5 control is an approximate preference mapping, not a measured rating.
  • Some numeric fields include zero values (for example engine size or fuel efficiency), which may mean missing/placeholder values. Check with the data provider before treating every zero as real.
  • Large prices flagged as outliers can be valid premium cars. Do not remove them without checking the records and deciding what market the model is meant to cover.
Generalization: These summary patterns describe this file. They do not prove what causes prices in the real market, and the data may not represent current local listings.

Predictive model: what it does

The HTML estimator uses a similarity-weighted nearest-neighbour approach. It searches the dataset for cars close to the entered brand/model, age, mileage, condition, fuel type, transmission and accident history. More similar examples get more weight; the displayed estimate is the weighted median, with a weighted 20th–80th percentile range.

This is a sensible first choice for a mixed dataset with both numeric and categorical details: it does not require categories to be turned into arbitrary numbers, and users can understand it as “compare this car with similar cars.” It is not a neural network or a random forest.

Comparing MSE and RMSE: MSE is in squared dollars, so $3.90 million is not a typical price error. It squares each miss before averaging, which makes a few large misses count heavily. RMSE takes the square root of MSE, returning the result to dollars ($1,974). RMSE is therefore much easier to explain as an error scale; MAE ($1,060) gives the average miss without squaring.

What R² says: R² = 0.987 indicates that, on this particular holdout, the model captured about 98.7% of the variation in listed prices compared with a baseline that predicts the test-set average for every car. It is not 98.7% accuracy, and it does not mean each valuation is correct to that degree. The remaining 1.3% is variation not explained by these predictions.

Important caveat: Validation uses a single random split from the same CSV, with the interface’s 1–5 control mapped to New=5, Used=3, Damaged=1. It is not a test on future listings or another market. The 1–5 match-quality stars are a heuristic based on match count/distance, not a calibrated probability that the estimate is correct. A separate validation sample, subgroup error checks, and external/current listings would be needed before relying on the estimate for a transaction.

Short peer briefing

“I analyzed 50,000 used-car listings with 25 fields. Prices are strongly right-skewed: the median is about $10,063, while the average is $15,781, with a standard deviation of about $16,839.”
“Age and mileage each have a correlation around −0.60 with price; horsepower is around +0.52. These are associations, and brand/model still matter a lot.”
“I built a nearest-comparable-cars estimator. In a random holdout of 1,000 rows it had an MSE of about $3.90 million squared dollars, RMSE of about $1,974, MAE of about $1,060, and R² of 0.987. That is a promising within-file result, but it does not prove the model will perform equally well on current real-world listings.”

Best next step: validate on a later-dated or independent dataset, then check errors separately by price band, brand, and condition before presenting the estimate as decision-grade.
